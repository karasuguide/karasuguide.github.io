Which of four paid-newsletter scenarios sounds like yours?

A. Starting from zero audience

Keep fixed costs low while validating the publication. Kit is worth comparing if its free email plan and pay-as-you-sell Commerce cover your launch workflow; include the per-sale fee and plan needed for advanced automation. Substack publishing is free but takes a share once you charge. beehiiv Free supports up to 2,500 subscribers; paid subscriptions with 0% platform fees are listed on Lite/Pro, so model that upgrade. None guarantees an audience.

B. Creator with an existing email list

Kit is a natural shortlist when your business relies on tags, segmented broadcasts, welcome sequences, and product offers. Confirm plan access and export fields. Consider beehiiv to consolidate publication, site, and audience reporting; test import, attribution, and list-tier pricing. Substack fits writer-led publishing and its reader network, but test what subscriber and paid-member data can move. Preserve consent and suppression records on any platform.

C. Paid newsletter as a primary business

Model plan costs and transaction fees at launch, break-even, and target scale. beehiiv Lite/Pro may compare well if the plan cost is offset by avoiding a platform share, but card processing remains. Kit charges per sale while email costs are separate. Substack has no publishing subscription, but its 10% share plus recurring-payment fees rise with revenue. Compare failed payments, refunds, cancellation, payouts, support, and migration of memberships/payment methods.

D. Newsletter plus products, services, or community

Kit merits a look when sales depend on email sequences and segments. beehiiv may consolidate its site, digital products, community, and newsletter. Substack can keep writing and reader recommendations together, but a separate store or service workflow may remain. Map signup to purchase and fulfillment, then compare integrations, permissions, and manual reconciliation.

Substack: simple publishing with a revenue share

Substack's current pricing help says publishing is free and Substack keeps 10% of paid subscription revenue. Payment charges are separate: its US examples list Stripe card processing at 2.9% + $0.30 per payment and Stripe Billing at 0.7% for recurring payments. Local payment methods, country, taxes, and in-app purchases can change the total. Treat these as published US examples, not a universal quote.

Substack may appeal if a straightforward writing and membership flow matters more than extensive site or marketing customization. Its network and recommendation surfaces can be part of a distribution plan, but they do not guarantee discovery. Check publication branding, analytics, customer communication, paid access controls, exports, and the practical steps for taking an audience elsewhere before depending on the platform as your whole business.

Pricing and paid-newsletter economics

Compare net receipts, not just the advertised plan. For each platform, estimate monthly paid transactions at launch, a break-even point, and a realistic target. Include:

  • Recurring platform and subscriber-tier charges
  • Percentage platform revenue share, if any
  • Payment processing, including any fixed amount per transaction
  • Refunds, failed payments, taxes, and currency conversion
  • Domains, integrations, paid add-ons, and migration assistance
  • Your time for manual operations and customer support

For a monthly $10 US card subscription, Substack's published examples imply $1.00 in platform fee, $0.59 in Stripe card processing, and $0.07 in Stripe Billing (0.7%), or about $1.66 total before taxes, refunds, or other costs. Kit's stated USD Commerce fee is $0.65 per $10 sale and is its stated only selling cost. beehiiv lists 0% platform fee on Lite/Pro; its published US Stripe card example is $0.59, plus the applicable subscriber-tier plan cost. These are fee illustrations from providers' published terms, not an earnings forecast; annual billing, countries, currencies, and payment methods can change the calculation.

For monthly plans, model one charge per active paid reader; annual billing changes the timing and amount of charges. Also consider churn, discounts, failed payments, and whether the platform's subscriber-tier pricing counts inactive records. Do not assume a platform feature will increase conversion or retention without evidence from your audience.

Test before you commit

  1. Import a small, consented sample contact set; check tags, custom fields, unsubscribes, and suppression status.
  2. Complete a signup and payment flow in an available test mode; verify receipts, access, and the paid-subscriber path.
  3. Trigger the welcome sequence and any paid onboarding or segmentation rules.
  4. Create and send a representative email to test editing, previews, mobile rendering, links, and web archive.
  5. Review analytics/reporting for acquisition source, delivery, engagement, paid conversions, and revenue; note definitions and plan limits.
  6. Export sample contacts and content; inspect fields, statuses, and formats, then document what does not transfer.
  7. Run cancellation, refund, and failed-payment scenarios; verify access removal, subscriber notices, and payout records.
  8. Calculate costs at launch, break-even, and target scale. Document roles, integrations, consent/security needs, and rollback steps before migration.

For a migration, retain consent and suppression records, authenticate sending domains carefully, and communicate any reader action in advance. Make a rollback plan before moving an established paid publication.

Frequently asked questions

Which platform is best for a paid newsletter?

There is no universal best choice. Compare your publishing workflow, fee model, audience tools, exports, and operational requirements, then test the reader journey on your shortlist.

Which platform has the lowest fees?

It depends on paid revenue, transaction size, subscriber count, and required plan. Substack lists a 10% platform share plus processing; Kit lists its Commerce transaction fee; beehiiv says it takes 0% of paid subscription revenue but has tiered software plans. Calculate all applicable costs for your own case.

Can I start without an existing audience?

Yes, but software cannot guarantee discovery or paid conversions. Start with a focused editorial promise, a manageable publishing cadence, and a way to learn from reader response before taking on unnecessary fixed costs.

Can I export my subscribers if I switch?

Kit and Substack document subscriber export options, and beehiiv documents subscriber and post exports. Check what fields and statuses are included and separately confirm how paid memberships and recurring payments can move; a CSV export alone may not transfer billing relationships. For a checklist on moving an existing newsletter from Substack, see the migration guide before switching.

Can I offer both free and paid content?

Each platform supports paid products or subscriptions in some form, but tier controls and delivery workflows differ. Build a test with your proposed free and paid reader paths and confirm the current plan requirements.

Does a platform's built-in network guarantee audience growth?

No. Recommendations or network distribution can provide possible discovery paths, but results depend on the publication, audience, and execution. Treat growth claims as hypotheses to measure rather than guaranteed outcomes.

Does a subscriber export move paid subscriptions?

No CSV export should be assumed to transfer a recurring billing relationship. Confirm with each provider how payment methods, active entitlements, consent, and cancellation records move, and whether subscribers must take action.

Final recommendation

Use beehiiv when bundled publishing tools and its tiered price fit; Kit when automation and creator commerce are central; Substack when writer-led simplicity and its network fit despite the revenue share. Confirm current fees, customization, exports, and payment migration with your pilot.
