Start with your publishing model

Before comparing individual features, define what the newsletter represents within your business. A creator publishing a free weekly email has different requirements from a membership business, a media startup, or a consultant using a newsletter to generate leads.

Ask these questions first

  • Is email the main product, or is it one channel within a broader website?
  • Will the publication be free, paid, sponsorship-funded, or supported by several revenue streams?
  • Who will write, edit, design, and manage subscribers?
  • Do you need one publication or expect to operate several?
  • Which growth workflows are essential at launch rather than merely desirable?
  • What subscriber, content, and analytics data must remain exportable?
  • How much technical administration can you handle every month?

Write down the answers before viewing plan pages. This prevents an impressive but peripheral capability from outweighing the workflows that determine whether the publication can run consistently.

Where beehiiv may fit better

beehiiv belongs on the shortlist when a creator wants newsletter operations, a publication website, growth, and monetization addressed within the same product environment. Consolidation can be valuable for a solo operator or lean team because every additional service creates another subscription, login, integration, failure point, and support relationship.

That does not mean every creator will need every capability. A simple newsletter can become harder to operate if the team adopts complex growth or monetization workflows before establishing a consistent publishing process. Evaluate beehiiv against the publication you expect to run over the next 12 to 24 months, not an imagined media business with requirements you may never reach.

During evaluation, create a sample issue, build the corresponding web page, model the subscriber journey, and outline the intended revenue workflow. Record which steps are native, which require configuration, and which depend on another service. This reveals more than a general feature checklist.

When Ghost should move ahead

Ghost should move ahead when its current offer scores better on the requirements that matter most to your business. Those could include your preferred publishing workflow, website specification, deployment approach, technical capacity, portability standards, or total operating cost.

The key is to turn those priorities into acceptance tests. Do not assume that two products using similar terminology implement a capability in the same way. For example, a feature may differ in configuration effort, design flexibility, user permissions, payment dependencies, analytics, or plan availability.

Ask for a demonstration of your actual workflow rather than a generic product tour. If you publish with collaborators, include the complete draft, review, approval, scheduling, and correction cycle. If revenue is central, model subscriber signup, payment, access changes, failed payments, cancellation, and financial reporting.

Pricing requires a total-cost calculation

The advertised subscription is only one part of platform cost. Current plan names, prices, limits, and billing terms can change, so calculate costs from each vendor’s live commercial terms at the time of purchase.

Your comparison should include:

  • The plan required for the capabilities you consider essential
  • How cost changes as the audience or team grows
  • Payment-processing and monetization-related charges
  • Third-party services needed for missing workflows
  • Website, domain, infrastructure, or technical administration costs
  • Migration, setup, design, and staff-training time
  • The cost of replacing a feature if you later change platforms

Create three scenarios: your current size, a realistic 12-month forecast, and a higher-growth case. A lower starting price can be misleading if a required capability forces an early upgrade or depends on several external tools.

Integrations and automation: test complete workflows

An integration should not receive credit merely because its name appears in a directory. Document what information must move, in which direction, how quickly, and what should happen when the connection fails.

For each platform, test the workflows that affect revenue or subscriber trust. Common examples include adding subscribers from forms, applying audience attributes, sending purchase data to a customer system, removing unsubscribed contacts, and transferring campaign results to reporting tools.

If you depend on API access, confirm authentication methods, available endpoints, rate or usage limits, webhooks, plan requirements, and the effort needed to maintain custom code. Creators without development support should also estimate the continuing cost of no-code automation services.

Migration and portability

Migration risk is easy to underestimate because a subscriber list is only one part of a publication. Content archives, URLs, tags, audience attributes, templates, automations, analytics history, payment relationships, and consent records may all affect continuity.

Before choosing either platform, request or perform representative exports. Inspect the file structure and confirm whether the data required to reconstruct the publication is included. Also determine which elements would need to be rebuilt manually.

A practical migration plan should cover:

  1. Inventorying content, subscribers, domains, integrations, and revenue workflows
  2. Cleaning data and documenting consent or suppression requirements
  3. Testing a small import and checking field mapping
  4. Rebuilding templates, forms, automations, and tracking
  5. Preparing domain and email-authentication changes
  6. Running pre-launch checks across desktop and mobile
  7. Monitoring delivery, subscriptions, payments, and analytics after launch

Even if you are launching a new publication, understanding the exit path is worthwhile. Portability is easier to evaluate before years of content and business processes accumulate.

Security, privacy, and operational questions

Creators hold subscriber information and may connect publishing systems to payment, analytics, and customer-management services. Review each platform’s current security and privacy documentation in the context of your legal obligations and risk tolerance.

At minimum, investigate account protections, user roles, data handling, subprocessors, backup and recovery expectations, incident communication, deletion procedures, and the controls available when a collaborator leaves. If you serve regulated customers or operate across jurisdictions, obtain appropriate professional guidance rather than relying on a marketing summary.
