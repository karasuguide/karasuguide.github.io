Migrating From Ghost to beehiiv: What Newsletter Publishers Should Evaluate Before Switching

A practical Ghost-to-beehiiv migration guide covering subscriber consent, content, memberships, websites, monetization, and launch-risk management.

Moving a newsletter is not simply a matter of transferring an email list. A publication may also depend on web archives, membership records, payment processes, forms, analytics, automations, domains, and search traffic. Each dependency creates a potential point of failure during a platform change.

beehiiv is built around newsletter creation and operations, website building, audience growth, and monetization. That focus may appeal to publishers who want more of their workflow in one platform. Before switching, however, Ghost publishers should determine whether beehiiv can preserve the parts of the business that matter—not just the subscriber count.

Start with the reason for moving

A migration should solve a specific operational or commercial problem. Otherwise, the publication takes on transition risk without a clear definition of success.

Write down the primary objective before comparing features. Common goals include:

  • Reducing the number of tools needed to publish and distribute a newsletter
  • Making audience-growth work easier to manage
  • Creating a workflow that non-technical team members can operate
  • Bringing newsletter publishing, a public website, and monetization into a more unified system
  • Replacing a setup that has become expensive or difficult to maintain

Turn that objective into measurable acceptance criteria. For example, a publisher might require uninterrupted sending, preservation of consent records, working paid memberships, stable branded-domain access, and no loss of important archive pages. These criteria become the basis for a go-or-no-go decision.

Inventory the existing Ghost publication

Before configuring another platform, document everything the current publication depends on. This prevents visible assets such as posts and subscribers from overshadowing less obvious dependencies.

Audience data

  • Active email subscribers
  • Unsubscribed, suppressed, bounced, or complained addresses
  • Free and paid member records
  • Tags, segments, custom fields, and acquisition sources
  • Consent dates and other evidence of permission
  • Engagement history used for segmentation

Content and web assets

  • Published posts and newsletter issues
  • Drafts, scheduled content, authors, and publication dates
  • Images, downloadable files, and embedded media
  • Page titles, descriptions, slugs, canonical settings, and internal links
  • Static pages such as About, Contact, Privacy, and Terms
  • Forms, landing pages, navigation, and branded-domain settings

Revenue and operations

  • Membership tiers and entitlement rules
  • Active subscriptions and payment dependencies
  • Sponsorship placements and campaign commitments
  • Automations, welcome sequences, and transactional messages
  • Analytics, attribution, and reporting workflows
  • Integrations, webhooks, API-based processes, and manual exports

Mark each item as essential, replaceable, archival, or safe to retire. Not every historical customization needs to survive, but every omission should be intentional.

Evaluate subscriber portability carefully

The mailing list is usually the highest-risk migration asset. A clean transfer needs to preserve both permission and exclusion data. Importing active addresses while overlooking unsubscribes or suppressions can create compliance problems and damage sender reputation.

Before committing to beehiiv, confirm the accepted import format, field mapping options, treatment of duplicate records, handling of suppressed contacts, and any review process applied to imported audiences. If tags or custom fields drive important workflows, create a field-by-field mapping document rather than assuming they will translate directly.

Also determine which historical data will not move. Past opens, clicks, acquisition attribution, and automation history may be operationally valuable even if they are not required to send the next issue. Export and retain appropriate records before closing or changing the old system.

Separate content migration from newsletter migration

Email distribution and website publishing are related, but they create different migration problems. A publication can transfer its subscribers successfully while still losing archive usability, search visibility, or important inbound links.

Create a content manifest containing every URL that matters. For each page or post, record:

  • Its current URL
  • The desired destination URL
  • Publication date and author
  • Images and other attached assets
  • Metadata worth preserving
  • Internal links that may need updating
  • Whether the page should be migrated, redirected, archived elsewhere, or retired

Test representative content before attempting the entire archive. Include long articles, image-heavy posts, embedded media, gated material, custom formatting, and posts with extensive internal links. This reveals formatting and structural problems while there is still time to change the plan.

Assess the future website, not just the email editor

beehiiv includes website-building capabilities, but a publisher still needs to decide whether the resulting site can support its particular publication model. A newsletter archive and a full editorial website do not always have the same requirements.

Review the pages, navigation, branding controls, domain setup, metadata, URL structure, forms, and legal content needed at launch. Publishers with custom templates, complex information architecture, or extensive evergreen content should make website fit a central part of the decision.

Domain changes also require coordination. Document DNS records before editing them, identify who controls the domain account, and plan how sending-related and website-related records will be validated. Avoid changing the domain, importing the full audience, and sending the first campaign at the same moment.

Protect membership and monetization continuity

If the publication earns revenue, treat the move as a billing and entitlement migration—not merely a content project. List every revenue stream and identify what must continue without interruption.

For paid memberships, investigate how existing subscribers, renewal dates, access rights, cancellations, refunds, taxes, and failed payments would be handled. Do not assume that transferring an email address also transfers a payment relationship or content entitlement.

For advertising and sponsorships, document contracted send dates, placement requirements, tracking links, reporting obligations, and invoice status. A platform transition should not make it harder to deliver or report on commitments already sold.

beehiiv provides monetization capabilities, but suitability depends on the publication's revenue model. Confirm the current plan requirements, fees, eligibility rules, payment flow, and geographic availability for any capability central to the business.

Rebuild the publication workflow explicitly

A migration can expose undocumented habits that previously held the operation together. Map the full path from idea to published newsletter:

  1. Drafting and editing
  2. Internal review and approval
  3. Audience selection
  4. Link and rendering checks
  5. Scheduling or sending
  6. Web publication
  7. Promotion and repurposing
  8. Performance reporting
  9. Sponsor or stakeholder reporting

Assign an owner to each stage and confirm that the proposed workflow works for every role. Editors may care about revision and approval processes, operators about segmentation and scheduling, and business owners about revenue and reporting.

Any external system should be reviewed separately. If the publication relies on an integration or API, confirm current availability, plan requirements, authentication, supported actions, and usage limits. Prepare a manual fallback for launch week where practical.

Use a staged migration plan

A reversible, staged rollout is safer than a single cutover.

Stage 1: Discovery

Complete the asset inventory, define success criteria, identify owners, and list all unresolved questions. Record baseline metrics so that post-launch results can be interpreted in context.

Stage 2: Prototype

Build the basic publication structure, configure branding, and migrate a small content sample. Use test records rather than the live audience wherever possible.

Stage 3: Data rehearsal

Export a representative subscriber sample, clean it, map fields, and inspect the resulting records. Test exclusions as carefully as active subscriptions.

Stage 4: Workflow validation

Create a complete issue and run it through drafting, review, segmentation, link checking, delivery, web publication, and reporting. Include the people who will operate the system after launch.

Stage 5: Controlled cutover

Freeze changes to the old system for a defined period, take final exports, reconcile record counts, update necessary domain settings, and send first to a controlled segment if the operating plan allows it.

Stage 6: Post-launch monitoring

Check subscriber states, exclusions, links, forms, website pages, domain behavior, membership access, and revenue workflows. Keep the old environment accessible until critical records and workflows have been validated.

When beehiiv may fit—and when to pause

beehiiv is worth evaluating when the operating model is centered on producing newsletters, maintaining a publication website, growing an audience, and developing monetization. Consolidating those priorities may be attractive to a small team seeking a more focused publishing workflow.

Pause the migration if essential subscriber states cannot be mapped, paid-member continuity remains unresolved, the website requirements have not been tested, or a critical integration lacks a workable replacement. Publishers with highly customized sites or complex operational dependencies should give those requirements more weight than convenience in the core newsletter workflow.

Final recommendation

Do not decide on the basis of a feature checklist alone. Build a representative version of the publication, test the hardest content and subscriber cases, map every revenue dependency, and rehearse the launch before moving the full audience.

A Ghost-to-beehiiv migration makes sense only when the new operating model is clearly better for the publication and the transition can be completed without compromising consent, access, revenue, or important web assets. If those conditions are not yet met, resolve the gaps before setting a cutover date.
